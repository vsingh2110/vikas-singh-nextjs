# The Last Free Humans: North Sentinel Island and the Trap We Call Progress

> Note: This article is available in both English and Hindi.
>
> The English version is original. The Hindi version is an AI-assisted translation.

> A word on what this is: part of this essay is well-established science, carefully sourced at the end. Part of it is interpretation, and part is frankly my own opinion. Where a claim is contested, I have tried to say so. The aim is not a settled verdict, but an honest mirror.

Somewhere in India's Bay of Bengal, inside the Andaman Islands, there is a piece of land that does not obey the modern world.

It is called North Sentinel Island. It is roughly 60 square kilometres of dense forest ringed by coral reef - about half the size of Chandigarh, or nearly the same size as Manhattan. And on it lives a people who have refused, again and again, to join the rest of us.

![North Sentinel Island seen from far out at sea, ringed by reef, no people visible](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/10-north-sentinel-island-no-contact-1600x900.jpg)

*Seen only from a respectful distance: an island whose inhabitants have chosen, over and over, to be left alone.*

The Sentinelese are believed to descend from people who reached the Andamans tens of thousands of years ago - some estimates say fifty to sixty thousand - making them among the most isolated human beings on Earth. Nobody knows their real number. Because counts are made from boats offshore, estimates range from about 50 to 200 people, likely in the low hundreds at most.

They have no hospital, no school, no temple, no mosque, no court, no police station, and no market. Even the "most backward" tribes elsewhere usually have some fixed hierarchy - a chief, a headman, an institution of marriage. The Sentinelese appear to live closer to how our earliest ancestors (early *Homo sapiens*) lived: in a small, mobile, sharing band, with no visible ruler and nothing that could be called private wealth.

I want to be careful here, because this island is easy to romanticize. So let me do two things at once. Let me tell you why that older way of living was, in real and measurable ways, healthier and freer than we admit. And let me tell you, just as honestly, what it cost - because it cost a great deal.

## Who we were before the fields

For roughly ninety-five percent of the human story, this band life was the human default. We were not settled. We walked.

But not the way legend claims. A popular line says our ancestors walked a hundred kilometres a day. They did not. GPS studies of the Hadza, a foraging people in Tanzania, report roughly seven to fourteen kilometres a day - about 12.9 km for men and 7.6 km for women - which is real, sustained travel, several times what a typical office worker moves, but nowhere near the myth. The point is not the distance. The point is that the body was in constant, varied, low-grade use: walking, digging, climbing, carrying, resting, and walking again.

![A small band walks single file across open grassland at dawn, tracking, carrying simple tools](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/01-foragers-tracking-savanna-1600x900.jpg)

*For most of the human story we moved in small bands, tracking rather than owning the land we crossed.*

What did that produce? Bodies that modern medicine finds almost startling. Contemporary hunter-gatherers like the Hadza are strikingly lean and metabolically healthy - obesity is under five percent, blood pressure stays low and flat across the whole lifespan, and type-2 diabetes and heart disease are rare. Interestingly, this is not simply because they "burn more" - one careful study found their total daily energy expenditure was similar to ours. Their health comes less from calories burned and more from what they never ate and never sat still for.

The clearest witness, though, is the skeleton. When researchers X-rayed the spongy bone inside the hip joint across seven thousand years of human remains from one region, they found that foragers had about twenty percent denser, stronger bone than the farmers who came later in the same place - a gap comparable to the bone loss astronauts suffer after months of weightlessness. Our fragile modern skeleton, it turns out, is a recent thing. It begins with farming.

This is roughly what the anthropologist Marshall Sahlins meant when he called foragers "the original affluent society" - affluent not because they had much, but because they wanted little, and could meet their wants in a few hours of food-getting a day. It is a beautiful idea. It is also only half true, and I will come back to the other half. Their food-getting hours were genuinely low; but once you count cooking, water, firewood, tool-making, and childcare, the total workload was substantial. Call it this: they had less drudgery and more unhurried time than the farmers who followed - not an effortless paradise.

There is an old Sanskrit word for the state they seem to have lived closer to than we do: contentment (संतोष, *santoṣa*) - wants that are limited and easily met. And there is a word for its opposite, the engine that never lets us rest: craving (तृष्णा, *tṛṣṇā*), the thirst that renews itself the moment it is satisfied. Hold on to both words. This whole essay turns on them.

## The cooperative hunt

These were not helpless people foraging berries in fear. When the moment demanded it, they took on animals many times their size.

The archaeology of the Clovis people in North America, from around thirteen thousand years ago, is associated with beautifully made stone spear points, and analysis of their tools has turned up the blood residues of mammoth, mastodon, horse, and bison. Bringing down a creature like that was not an act of individual heroism. It "would have taken a group of hunters" - coordinated, close-range, sharing the danger and then sharing the meat. (Whether Clovis people hunted or mostly scavenged such giants is genuinely debated, so I will say only: the evidence points to coordinated big-game hunting.)

![Silhouetted hunters fan out across a cold misty plain toward a large tusked megafauna](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/02-megafauna-coordinated-hunt-1600x900.jpg)

*Bringing down megafauna demanded cooperation, not command - shared risk, and shared meat.*

There was another way we hunted, one written into our bodies: endurance. The "endurance running" hypothesis holds that our species evolved to run prey into heat exhaustion - jogging after an antelope for hours in midday sun until it collapses while we, cooled by sweat, keep going. In the Kalahari, such persistence hunts have been documented covering twenty-five to thirty-five kilometres in a single chase. (Note: that is a rare, spectacular event - do not confuse it with the eight-to-fourteen kilometre daily average.)

And yet - and this matters for our pride - we were also the hunted. Fossil evidence shows early humans ending up as prey: two *Homo habilis* individuals from Olduvai Gorge carry the bite marks of a leopard. A lone human on that landscape was desperately vulnerable. A united group could deter almost anything. This is the oldest lesson our species ever learned, and the one we are busy unlearning: there is safety, and sanity, in numbers.

## The trap we call progress

Then, around twelve thousand years ago, we did something that changed everything - and not, for the individual, entirely for the better.

We began to farm.

The historian Yuval Noah Harari calls the Agricultural Revolution "history's biggest fraud." His inversion is unforgettable: **we did not domesticate wheat; wheat domesticated us.** The free wanderer, who slept under a new sky each night, tied himself to a single patch of ground - and while he thought he was capturing the land, the land was quietly capturing him. Jared Diamond, a geographer and physiologist, went further and called farming "the worst mistake in the history of the human race."

![A lone figure bent over a small tilled plot at dusk, one ankle tethered to the soil](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/04-bound-to-the-plot-agriculture-trap-1200x1500.jpg)

*Agriculture fed far more people, but chained each of them to one patch of ground - a harvest to guard, a field never to leave.*

Why call a triumph a fraud? Because agriculture pulled off a cruel trick. It multiplied the total amount of food, and so it multiplied us - populations boomed. But for the average human being, the deal was bad. Diets narrowed to a few starchy cereals. Skeletons from early farming villages show more malnutrition markers, more anaemia, more infectious lesions, worn teeth, and in many regions people simply got shorter. The endless, repetitive labour of tilling and grinding wore down spines and joints in ways foraging rarely had.

And there was a new horror that came with the animals we penned beside us. Many of our great killer diseases are gifts from domestication and crowding: measles is a close cousin of cattle rinderpest; smallpox, influenza, and others crossed over from the beasts we kept and the dense, dirty settlements we built. A recent study of over a thousand ancient genomes found that these "crowd" diseases first appear only around 6,500 years ago and peak around 5,000 years ago - exactly as we took up herding and settled living.

So here is the trade, stated plainly. Agriculture was a triumph for the *species* and a demotion for the *person*. More of us; worse-off, each of us. That gap - between what is good for the population and what is good for the individual - is the crack through which almost everything else in this essay falls.

## From band to self: the birth of the ego

Something deeper than diet changed when we settled. I think the self was born.

When you belong to a mobile band that shares everything, the primary unit of experience is the group. You are a "we" before you are an "I." But the moment a man owns a field and the grain that grows on it, he feels, perhaps for the first time, the hard edge of a separate self - the bare "I" (अहम्, *aham*). And from that "I-maker" grows the whole apparatus of ego.

The father of modern psychoanalysis, Sigmund Freud, gave this apparatus a famous map: the *id* (in his German, *das Es*, "the It") - raw instinct and appetite; the *ego* (*das Ich*, "the I") - the manager that negotiates with reality; and the *superego* (*das Über-Ich*, "the Over-I") - the internalized voice of society and its rules. A creature of pure appetite becomes, through the pressure of ownership and society, a full personality. The human (*Homo sapiens*) becomes a person.

Indian philosophy mapped this same terrain thousands of years earlier, and reached the opposite conclusion about what to do with it. In the Samkhya (सांख्य) system, the ego has a precise name: the "I-maker" (अहंकार, *ahaṃkāra*), the faculty that manufactures the sense of a bounded, separate self. In Vedanta (वेदांत), a sharp line is drawn between that ego and the true Self, the pure witnessing awareness behind it - the Self (आत्मन्, *ātman*). Here is the striking difference: Freud's therapy aims to build a *stronger* ego, better at coping. The Indian systems aim at liberation (मोक्ष, *mokṣa*) by seeing *through* the ego, dissolving the "I-maker" entirely.

I am not claiming these are the same theory - the resemblance between Freud's ego and *ahaṃkāra* is an evocative analogy, not an equivalence, and there is no line of influence between them. But notice what both are pointing at: a constructed "I," and the suspicion that this "I" is the source of most of our trouble. The Sentinelese, if the anthropology is right, never had to build that fortress of self. They stayed, in a sense, a "we."

## Property and its children: marriage, patriarchy, inheritance

Once you can store wealth, you have to answer a question no wandering band ever needed to ask: **who gets it when I die?**

That single question, I think, reshaped the human family. The philosopher Friedrich Engels argued, in *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*, that patriarchal monogamous marriage arose largely to guarantee that property passed to a man's *own* biological heirs. To be sure the child was his, a man needed certainty of fatherhood - and so, Engels wrote, came the control and seclusion of women, in what he called, with real weight, "the world-historic defeat of the female sex."

![A low fence and a guarded grain store divide owned land from the open wild beyond](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/05-first-fences-grain-store-property-1600x900.jpg)

*A wall, a store of grain, a line drawn in the dirt: the surplus that now had to be owned, counted, and defended.*

Where women may once have held wide influence, in many agrarian societies that influence was cut in half, because a man wanted to be *sure* the children carrying his land were his blood. And because muscle now translated into land, sons became assets: more sons meant more hands, more force, more territory held. Some men took more than one woman to produce more children; some simply took women by force. Blood and abduction became routine.

I should be honest that this is a theory, and a contested one. Engels leaned on a nineteenth-century idea - an original "matriarchy" that agriculture overthrew - that most anthropologists now reject. (Matrilineal descent, tracing family through the mother, is real and documented; matriarchy, women *ruling* men, is not.) But the core intuition is taken seriously by serious economists: heritable, storable surplus creates a powerful incentive to control reproduction and lock down ownership. Farming did not invent every hierarchy - some societies had property and even slavery without it - but it was a mighty amplifier.

![Symbolic still life: a carved lineage in stone, a locked chest, joined rings, a grain measure](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/06-patriarchy-marriage-inheritance-1200x1500.jpg)

*Once wealth could be stored, it had to be passed down a chosen line - and marriage and inheritance became instruments of control.*

## Caste, religion, and the machinery of order

A field divides people into two kinds: those who own it, and those who work it. To hold that inequality in place, you need machinery. You need rules that feel larger than any king.

So the ranks hardened. In India, the earliest text naming the four social orders - the classes (वर्ण, *varṇa*) - is a Vedic hymn in which Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shudra are born from a single cosmic body. Many historians link the *hardening* of these ranks to the agrarian surplus that could feed non-farming priests and warriors who lived off tribute. Genetic evidence even dates the shift to rigid, hereditary intermarriage boundaries - the biological signature of caste - to roughly two to four thousand years ago.

![A stepped temple-pyramid at dusk, tiny figures in tiers from broad base to lit summit](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/07-hierarchy-caste-religion-pyramid-1200x1500.jpg)

*Stored surplus needed rulers, priests, and ranks - society hardened into tiers, a few at the lit summit and the many at the shadowed base.*

Organized religion, too, may have grown partly as social glue. One serious line of research argues that belief in powerful, morally watchful, punishing gods co-evolved with large societies of strangers, binding them into cooperation and helping stabilize property and order. I say "may," and I mean it: the direction of cause here is genuinely unresolved - a prominent study claiming complex societies came *before* such gods was later retracted. What is clear is that the security a settled man sought - safety, order, a guarantee - arrived wearing the chains of caste and creed. He asked for protection, and was handed a cage that called itself a home.

## Blood over grain, gold, and land

There is a blunt old proverb in the subcontinent for what men fight over: gold, woman, and land (जर, जोरू, ज़मीन, *zar, joru, zamīn*). Grain and gold, women, and territory. Sometimes one of the three lights the fire; sometimes two; sometimes all three at once. And the fire has essentially never gone out.

![Silhouetted ranks advance across a burning field toward a walled granary town, smoke rising](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/08-war-over-grain-gold-land-1600x900.jpg)

*Surplus worth taking is surplus worth fighting for: organized war arrives with the storehouse and the wall.*

You will read that humanity has fought "five thousand major wars." That number has no real source, so I will not lean on it. I will lean instead on the historian Will Durant, who wrote that in the last 3,421 years of recorded history, only 268 have seen no war. Even that is an estimate. But it makes the point that a precise count cannot: war is not our exception. Since the granary and the wall, it has been closer to our climate.

Modern scholars rightly warn against blaming everything on "resource wars" - ideology, fear, honour, and identity all pull the trigger too. But strip the slogans away and, underneath a startling number of conflicts, you find someone's grain, someone's gold, someone's land - and, in the ugliest of them, women counted as property alongside the rest. The storehouse we built for safety became the thing most worth killing for.

## The hedonic treadmill

Now we arrive at the strangest cost of all - one you can feel in your own chest today.

Even after all this progress - the surplus, the cities, the medicine, the endless goods - we are not, on average, more content. Psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell named the reason in 1971: the **hedonic treadmill**. We adapt. After a gain or a loss, we drift back toward a baseline. The famous, often-quoted study reported that lottery winners, some time later, were not much happier, and people paralyzed in accidents not much less happy, than everyone else. (That study is old and its drama outruns its statistics - and modern research shows adaptation is real but *partial*; some wounds never fully heal, and a steady baseline can itself be moved by insecurity and inequality. So: a strong tendency, not an iron law.)

![Diptych: a figure climbs an endless staircase of possessions, versus one resting by a warm fire](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/09-hedonic-treadmill-vs-contentment-1600x900.jpg)

*One life chases the next acquisition up a staircase that never ends; the other, by the fire, has already arrived.*

Why are we built this way? Look at the chemistry. Neuroscience distinguishes *wanting* from *liking* - and dopamine, it turns out, drives mostly the *wanting*, the pull toward a reward, more than the actual pleasure of having it. Worse, dopamine responds to *surprise*: it fires for a reward better than expected, and falls silent once that reward becomes routine. This is why the first bite beats the tenth, why a raise thrills you for a month and then becomes your new normal, why the anticipation is so often sweeter than the arrival.

Picture a hunter who brings down a great animal. For days, euphoria. Then the feeling fades - and it *must* fade, or he would sit content and starve while the next hunt goes unmade. The treadmill is not a design flaw. It is survival machinery. It exists to keep the organism striving.

Here is the twist that this whole essay has been walking toward. **That machinery still runs inside the Sentinelese. It runs inside all of us. What changes is only where it points.** In a band with no wage ladder, no property to pile up, and strong norms that mock the show-off and force sharing, the striving is aimed at survival and simple pleasures - a good hunt, a shared meal, respect earned by generosity or skill. In a market society, the very same craving is aimed at an open-ended staircase of money, status, and positional goods that can never, by design, be climbed to the top. The economist's "Easterlin paradox" is this treadmill at the scale of a whole nation: everyone's income rises together, the ranking stays put, and the promised happiness quietly washes out. We are still "keeping up with the Joneses," and the Joneses are always one step up.

The Sentinelese did not defeat the treadmill. They were simply never handed the one we run on. They kept, if the accounts of their neighbours are any guide, something we have traded away: a baseline that says *enough*. This is the difference between hedonic pleasure - the next hit - and what psychologists call eudaimonic well-being: meaning, belonging, purpose, the deep goods that do not habituate the way a new purchase does.

## But was it paradise? The honest ledger

Now I have to keep my promise and turn the page over, because everything above can curdle into a lie if I stop here.

It was not paradise. Loving a child in that world meant knowing, with terrible odds, that the child might not live. Across foraging societies, roughly forty percent of children did not survive to adolescence. Life expectancy at birth was around thirty - but read that number carefully, because it is a trick of arithmetic. It was dragged down by dead children. Those who survived childhood often lived into their sixties and seventies without a single doctor. Still: to be a parent there was to gamble, constantly, with the thing you loved most.

And death did not need a war or a beast. It could arrive through a scratch. Without antibiotics, a thorn in the foot or a cut on the hand could seed a lethal infection. Tetanus, whose spores live in ordinary soil, often kills without modern treatment, and in newborns it almost always does. A minor wound, an infected tooth, a difficult birth - any of these could be a death sentence, faced without a hospital to run to. They did have deep herbal knowledge, real and hard-won; but a poultice cannot stop sepsis once it reaches the blood.

The romantic picture fails in the body, too. Take the Jarawa, a neighbouring Andaman people. A government doctor who worked with them found them healthier than almost any mainland community - free of obesity, diabetes, and heart disease. True. And also: they suffered skin ulcers, hookworm, crocodile injuries, and carried one of the highest rates of hepatitis-B ever recorded, over half the tribe. Foragers escape the diseases of excess. They do not escape the body's older enemies.

Nor were these societies always gentle. The evidence for prehistoric violence is real; the "noble savage" living in perfect peace is a fantasy in the other direction. Honesty cuts both ways.

There is one more thing the ledger records, and it is the darkest, and it is the whole reason the Indian government keeps everyone away. Because the Sentinelese have been isolated so long, they are believed to have almost no immunity to our everyday germs. When their Andaman neighbours were contacted, disease did what spears could not: the Great Andamanese population collapsed by something like ninety-nine percent, the Onge by about eighty-five, and one small people, the Jangil, vanished entirely. A single visitor with a common cold could end a civilization that has survived for tens of thousands of years. Their isolation is not backwardness. It is, quite literally, life support.

So the honest verdict is not "they are happier than us." It is something stranger and sadder: *high emotional harmony, at terribly high physical stakes.* A life of deep belonging and few anxieties, lived one infection away from the end.

## North Sentinel today

Knowing all this, India made a choice that I think is wise: leave them completely alone.

Under a 1956 law protecting the Andaman tribes, all travel within about five nautical miles - roughly nine kilometres - of the island is banned. The policy is "eyes on, hands off." The old expeditions that dropped gifts on the beach were abandoned in the 1990s. The world's response to the last free humans is, finally, to stop trying to save them from their freedom.

They defend that freedom fiercely. In 2004, after the great tsunami, they fired arrows at the helicopters sent to check on them - and the fact that they had survived was the answer. In 2018, an American missionary who paid fishermen to smuggle him ashore, meaning to convert them, was killed on the beach; the tragedy was not only his death but that he could have carried in the very germs that might have wiped them out. Their archery is precise, their aim on moving boats accurate. They cannot smelt metal, but they cold-forge iron washed up from shipwrecks into tools and arrowheads. With no internet, no contraception, no market, and no economist advising them, their population has stayed in balance with an island of sixty square kilometres for longer than any nation on Earth has existed.

## What the island is trying to tell us

I do not want to go back. Let me say that clearly, because this kind of essay is always in danger of ending in a fantasy of return. I like antibiotics. I like that my children will almost certainly outlive their childhoods. I would not trade modern medicine, literacy, or the abolition of that forty-percent gamble for any amount of forest freedom. And in any case, the door is shut: we literally cannot walk onto that island without risking the murder of everyone on it. There is no going back, for them or for us.

![Diptych: a figure walks free along an open dusk shoreline, versus one dwarfed by grey towers](/images/blogs-images/last-free-humans-north-sentinel/11-freedom-vs-modern-anxiety-1600x900.jpg)

*Two ways to be human: unhurried under an open sky, or hemmed in by the walls we built and now must feed.*

But the island is a mirror, and mirrors are useful even when you cannot climb into them.

Here is what it shows. On the road we call progress, we gained almost everything: numbers, comfort, medicine, knowledge, the near-conquest of the childhood death that once ruled our species. And somewhere in the gaining, we quietly set down two things and never picked them back up. One was the "we" - the band that shared without counting, the safety and sanity of numbers, the absence of the fortress-self. The other was contentment (संतोष, *santoṣa*) - a baseline that could say *enough* and mean it, instead of a treadmill that redefines "enough" one rung higher every time we climb.

The Sentinelese cannot teach us to abandon our world; that would be a lie, and a dangerous one. But they can remind us of a distinction we have almost forgotten: that the machinery of craving inside us is not our master unless we let it be. We cannot leave the market. We can, perhaps, stop mistaking its staircase for a destination.

The last free humans are not free because they lack our tools. They are free because they were never taught to want without end.

That, at least, is a freedom we could still choose - without a boat, without an arrow, and without leaving home.
